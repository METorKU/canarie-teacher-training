## Band 8 model answer
Some people argue that universities should allow students to study any subject they choose, whereas others believe that higher education should concentrate on subjects that are likely to be useful in the future. Although science and technology deserve substantial support, I believe students should retain broad freedom of choice.

Those who favour a more restricted system have understandable concerns. Countries need engineers, medical professionals and technology specialists to sustain economic growth and respond to challenges such as ageing populations and climate change. Since many universities receive public funding, governments may reasonably wish to direct some places towards areas with serious skills shortages. Graduates in these fields can also benefit from strong employment prospects.

However, limiting university study to immediately practical subjects would overlook the contribution of the arts and humanities. History, philosophy and literature develop analytical thinking, research skills and the ability to communicate persuasively. These abilities are valuable in business, public service and many other sectors. Furthermore, students who are genuinely interested in their discipline are more likely to persist and perform well. Forcing a gifted artist or writer into a technical course could produce neither a committed scientist nor a successful creative professional.

In my view, universities should offer a wide range of courses while helping applicants make informed decisions. They could publish employment data, provide careers advice and ensure that students understand the financial implications of their choices. This approach respects individual talent without ignoring labour-market needs.

In conclusion, science and technology should be strongly supported, but university education should not be confined to them. Freedom of choice, combined with reliable career guidance, is the most balanced policy.
